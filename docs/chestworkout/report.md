> **Scope.** Chest (pectoralis major) training only, for a beginner or early-stage lifter in a well-equipped commercial gym. Not a full-body program, and not nutrition advice. Nothing here is medical advice; persistent or sharp joint pain needs a qualified professional.

# 1. Executive summary and primary conclusion

**The answer in one paragraph.** A beginner who wants to develop the whole pectoralis major should learn **(1) a low-incline press (≈15–30°, machine or dumbbells)** and **(2) a pec deck (or mid-height cable fly)** first. If they have time for a third exercise, add **(3) a horizontal (flat) chest-press machine**. If they can only do one exercise, it should be the **horizontal chest-press machine**. The flat and incline presses provide the most-studied stimulus. The pec deck adds a different pattern (shoulder horizontal adduction without the triceps limiting the set) and has direct evidence of growing both the upper and the mid-chest. All three are easy to learn, can be loaded in small steps, need no spotter and come with stops or a simple setup.

**How confident is this?**

| Conclusion | Evidence type | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pressing (bench, push-up, machine) grows the pectoralis major in beginners | Direct (several RCTs, MRI/ultrasound) | **High** |
| Free weights and machines produce similar overall hypertrophy | Direct (meta-analysis, mostly not chest-specific) | **Moderate–high** |
| Incline pressing shifts *some* growth toward the clavicular (upper) head | Direct but thin (one 8-week RCT in untrained men) plus EMG and acute swelling | **Moderate–low** |
| A fly/pec-deck movement grows both upper and mid-chest | Direct (one 12-week within-subject study) | **Moderate** |
| Incline + pec deck covers the mid and lower chest about as well as adding a flat press | Expert inference from the above | **Low–moderate** |
| "Inner" and "outer" chest can be trained independently | Not supported | — |

**Bottom line on certainty.** The *categories* (press + fly, flat + incline) rest on reasonable evidence. The *exact ordering* of similar machines, dumbbells and cables does not. Scores of 7.0 and 7.3 in this report are ties.

# 2. Anatomical map of the chest and its terminology

## 2.1 Pectoralis major

| Region | Origin | Fibre direction (arm at side) | Movements it contributes most to | Practical emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Clavicular head** ("upper chest") | Medial half of the clavicle | Runs downward-outward to the humerus | Shoulder flexion + horizontal adduction (pressing *up and in*) | Incline press ~15–45°, low-to-high cable fly |
| **Sternocostal head** ("mid chest", about 80% of the muscle's volume) | Sternum and upper 6 costal cartilages | Roughly horizontal (upper fibres) to upward-outward (lower fibres) | Horizontal adduction, and extension from a flexed position | Flat press, flat fly, pec deck |
| **Abdominal part** (inferior fibres, "lower chest") | Aponeurosis of the external oblique | Steeply upward-outward | Adduction/extension of the arm from overhead toward the hip | Decline press, dips, high-to-low cable fly |

All parts converge on a single, twisted, U-shaped tendon on the lateral lip of the bicipital groove of the humerus. The lower sternal fibres form a *deeper* layer that inserts higher on the humerus than the clavicular fibres ([Radiopaedia](https://images.radiopaedia.org/articles/pectoralis-major?lang=us)). The clavicular and sternocostal heads have different nerve supply, mostly the lateral versus medial pectoral nerves, and can be activated somewhat independently (Haley & Zacchilli, 2014). This is the anatomical basis for limited regional emphasis.

**How angle changes loading.** As the bench inclines, the line of force moves toward the clavicular fibres, so clavicular EMG rises while sternocostal and lower EMG falls. Anterior-deltoid involvement rises steadily with incline and becomes dominant at about 45–60° (Rodríguez-Ridao et al., 2020). Cable direction works the same way. Pulling *up and in* (low-to-high) lines up with clavicular fibres, and pulling *down and in* (high-to-low) lines up with the lower sternal and abdominal fibres. This last point is biomechanical inference. Neither cable direction has been tested for long-term regional growth.

## 2.2 Gym terms compared with anatomy

| Term | Anatomical? | What it really means | Can it be targeted? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper chest | **Yes**: clavicular head | A real, separately innervated region | **Partly**: incline work shifts emphasis, but every press trains it to some degree |
| Middle chest | **Mostly yes**: upper/mid sternocostal fibres | The bulk of the muscle | Trained by almost every chest exercise |
| Lower chest | **Yes**: lower sternocostal and abdominal fibres | The lower border and the "shelf" | Flat and decline pressing and dips favour it (EMG and acute swelling only) |
| Inner chest | **No** | The medial ends of all fibres near the sternum. Its look depends on overall muscle size, body fat and sternal shape. | **No.** A muscle fibre shortens along its whole length, so you cannot grow only its medial end. "Squeeze" movements do not isolate it. |
| Outer chest | **No** | The lateral fibres near the armpit and the visual "width" | **No.** Driven by overall size and the stretched position, not a separate muscle |
| Chest thickness | Appearance | How far the muscle sticks out from the ribcage, i.e. overall hypertrophy | Overall chest volume and progressive overload |
| Width/fullness | Appearance | Overall size plus clavicular development, and shoulder width from skeletal frame | Overall hypertrophy plus some upper-chest emphasis |

**Limits of regional targeting.** Uneven (regional) growth within a muscle is well documented, but we cannot yet reliably predict which exercise grows which region ([Stronger by Science, "Is regional hypertrophy predictable?"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/regional-hypertrophy/)). Treat regional emphasis as a *tilt*, not isolation.

## 2.3 Supporting muscles (only where they affect exercise choice)

- **Anterior deltoid.** It assists every press and takes over more as the incline gets steeper. It is the main reason not to go above roughly 30–45°.
- **Triceps brachii.** It extends the elbow in every press and often limits the set before the pecs do, especially with narrow grips. Fly movements remove it from the equation, which is the main argument for adding a fly.
- **Pectoralis minor and serratus anterior.** These are scapular muscles that position the shoulder blade. Push-ups let the shoulder blades move freely, while bench pressing pins them. They matter for comfort and setup, not as chest-growth targets. Bench pressing also grows pectoralis minor (Lanza et al., 2024).

# 3. Research method and evidence hierarchy

## 3.1 Research questions

| Question | Outcome | Evidence that would settle it | Most relevant studies | Main limitations | Decision it informs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Physiological effectiveness** | Long-term pec size (MRI, ultrasound thickness, CSA) | RCTs comparing exercises with effort and sets equated | Longitudinal training studies, meta-analyses | Few chest-specific head-to-head trials. Most are short (4–12 weeks) and use untrained young men. | Hypertrophy ranking (Table A) |
| **B. Beginner effectiveness** | Ability to apply A in practice: learning, stability, progression, tolerance | Adherence, technique and injury data in novices | Mostly practical/expert evidence and stability research | Almost no direct trials. This rests largely on expert inference. | Beginner ranking (Table B) |
| **C. Selection efficiency** | Extra, non-redundant stimulus added by an exercise in a combination | Studies comparing combinations, plus regional growth data | Chaves et al. 2020 (combined group), regional studies | Very limited. Mostly inference. | Combination ranking (Section 9) |

## 3.2 Evidence hierarchy used

1. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of long-term hypertrophy
2. Randomized longitudinal studies measuring pectoralis major size
3. Studies of *regional* pectoralis major growth
4. Controlled comparisons of exercise variations
5. Volume, load, effort, frequency and range-of-motion research (mostly not chest-specific)
6. Biomechanics
7. EMG (supplementary only)
8. Professional instructional guidance (ACE, NASM) for execution

**Each key claim is tagged** **[D]** direct (longitudinal hypertrophy), **[I]** indirect (EMG, acute swelling, biomechanics) or **[E]** expert inference. Acute EMG amplitude is never treated as proof of long-term growth, because it is not a validated predictor of hypertrophy (Vigotsky et al., 2022). A non-significant difference is never treated as proof that two exercises are identical.

Sources searched: PubMed and Europe PMC records, journal and DOAJ pages, preprint servers (SportRxiv), and evidence-review sites used only to locate primary studies. Every study in the register (Section 13) was checked against a bibliographic record. Anything not fully verified is labelled as such.

# 5. Region-by-region assessment

| Objective | Leading candidates | Anatomical rationale | Direct hypertrophy evidence | Supporting (indirect) evidence | Limitations | Reasonable claim | Is a separate exercise justified? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Whole pec major** | Flat or incline press (any equipment), push-up, pec deck | Every pec region crosses the shoulder | **[D]** Bench press grew pec major CSA by MRI (Lanza et al., 2024). Push-up = bench at matched load (Kikuchi & Nakazato, 2017). Pec deck grew both heads (Pinto et al., 2025). | — | Short studies, mostly untrained men | Strong | One good press covers most of it |
| **2. Upper chest** | Low-incline press (15–30°), machine incline press | Clavicular fibres line up with an upward-inward push | **[D]** In 8 weeks the incline-only group gained more upper-chest thickness than flat-only or mixed groups (Chaves et al., 2020) | **[I]** Clavicular EMG peaks near 30°, while front-delt EMG climbs at 45–60° (Rodríguez-Ridao et al., 2020). Incline produced greater acute clavicular CSA swelling (Albarello et al., 2022). | One small RCT in untrained men, 1 session/week. The size of the effect is suspiciously large. | Moderate–low: incline *tilts* growth toward the upper chest | **Yes.** This is the most defensible regional addition. |
| **3. Mid (sternocostal) chest** | Flat press (machine, DB, barbell), pec deck | Upper and mid sternal fibres line up with horizontal adduction | **[D]** Bench press and pec deck grow it (Lanza 2024, Pinto 2025). Chaves 2020 found no flat-vs-incline difference at the other measured sites. | **[I]** Sternal EMG highest at 0° (Rodríguez-Ridao 2020) | Few measurement sites per study | Moderate | Trained by almost any chest exercise |
| **4. Lower chest** | Flat press, decline press, dips, high-to-low cable fly | Lower sternal/abdominal fibres line up with downward-inward adduction | **None** comparing decline with flat for long-term growth | **[I]** Lower-region EMG falls as incline rises (Rodríguez-Ridao 2020). Flat bench produced larger acute sternal swelling than incline (Albarello 2022). | No longitudinal data | Low | **Usually no.** Flat pressing already loads these fibres. A decline press or high-to-low fly is an *optional* extra. |
| **5. Across fibre regions** | A press + a fly, and flat + incline | Regions differ in line of pull and innervation | **[D]** Pec deck grew clavicular (~17–18%) and sternocostal (~21%) thickness similarly (Pinto 2025) | — | One study, untrained | Moderate | A press/fly pair or a flat/incline pair is enough |
| **6. Useful range of motion** | DB press, machine press with full depth, pec deck, cable fly | Growth tends to be better when muscles are trained through long lengths | **[D]** Full ROM was trivially better overall, and lengthened partials *may* help (Wolf et al., 2023 meta-analysis, not chest-specific) | — | Pec-specific ROM trials are scarce | Moderate (general) | No. Use a full, comfortable range on whatever you choose. |
| **7. "Inner" chest look** | Overall chest growth | Not a separate region | None | — | — | Cannot be isolated | **No.** "Squeeze" exercises have no special effect. |
| **8. "Outer" chest look** | Overall growth, stretch-position loading | Not a separate region | None | — | — | Cannot be isolated | **No** |
| **9. Thickness/fullness** | Progressive pressing + fly volume | Overall CSA | **[D]** Volume dose-response (Pelland et al., 2026) | — | Not chest-specific | Strong (general) | No. Volume and progression matter more than exercise choice. |

**Can a small set cover the pec major?** Yes. One incline press plus one fly or flat press covers the clavicular, sternocostal and (indirectly) lower regions through two distinct movement patterns. A dedicated lower-chest or inner/outer exercise is not needed for a beginner.

# 6. Equipment and movement-pattern comparisons

| Comparison | Direct hypertrophy evidence | Practical verdict for a beginner |
|---|---|---|
| **Barbell vs dumbbell press** | No chest-specific long-term comparison found | Similar growth is likely **[E]**. Dumbbells give more range and a freer path but need more coordination and come in larger weight jumps. The barbell gives the finest loading but needs safety arms or a spotter. |
| **Dumbbells vs machine press** | **[D]** Meta-analysis of 13 studies: free weights ≈ machines for hypertrophy (SMD −0.06, *p* = 0.75). Strength gains were specific to the mode trained (Haugen et al., 2023). | **Machine wins for beginners** on ease and safe failure. Dumbbells win on range and individual path. |
| **Selectorized vs plate-loaded machine** | None | Selectorized: smaller jumps and quicker changes. Plate-loaded: often a better strength curve and range. Pick the one that fits you. |
| **Converging vs fixed-path machine** | None | Converging arms follow a natural hand path and often feel better at the shoulder **[E]**. No growth data. |
| **Press vs fly** | **[D]** Both grow the pec (Lanza 2024; Pinto 2025). Head-to-head DB press vs DB fly study announced (Schoenfeld group, accepted in *JSCR*, 2026); results not verified here. **[I]** The press shows higher average EMG, the fly loads the stretch with little tension at the top (Solstad et al., 2020). | **Complementary.** A press gives heavy loading and easy progression. A fly removes the triceps limit. |
| **Cable fly vs dumbbell fly** | None | Cable is easier to progress, keeps tension near the top and allows any line of pull. DB fly loads only the bottom and is riskier at deep stretch. **Prefer cable or pec deck.** |
| **Flat vs incline vs decline** | **[D]** Incline > flat for upper-chest thickness; other sites similar (Chaves 2020, untrained). Decline: no long-term data. | **Include a low incline.** Decline is optional. |
| **Bodyweight vs external load** | **[D]** Push-up ≈ bench press for pec thickness at 40% 1RM-matched load over 8 weeks (Kikuchi & Nakazato, 2017). A 4-week trial found neither group grew, so it was too short to tell (Kotarsky et al., 2018). | Push-ups work if taken close to failure and progressed. Progression is harder once you can do many reps. |
| **Bilateral vs unilateral** | None for the chest | Unilateral work helps with imbalances or a sore side. It doubles session time. Not needed by default. |
| **Free weights vs machines for beginners** | Haugen 2023 (above) | Neither is intrinsically superior for growth. Machines remove the coordination bottleneck, so a beginner can reach productive effort sooner **[E]**. |
| **Needs a spotter vs solo** | — | Barbell and heavy DB sets to near-failure need safety arms, a spotter or a conservative RIR. Machines, Smith (with catches), cables and push-ups can be taken close to failure alone. |

**No machine is universally safer, and no free weight universally builds more muscle.** Fit to the person matters more than equipment category.

# 8. Beginner-friendly weighted ranking (Table B)

## 8.1 Criteria and how each was scored

| Criterion | Weight | What it measures (no double counting) |
|---|---:|---|
| Hypertrophy potential | 30% | Score from Table A |
| Ease of learning and execution | 15% | How quickly a novice performs reps that look the same every time |
| Stability and coordination | 10% | How much balance and stabilizing the exercise demands (higher = less demand) |

| Useful and tolerable ROM | 10% | Whether the exercise allows a full, loaded range, especially in the stretched position |

| Setup simplicity | 5% | Time and steps to start the first set |

| Long-term practicality and adherence | 5% | Availability, boredom and scalability over years |

## 9.2 Each candidate in brief

- **C1 Horizontal + incline machine press.** Flat emphasizes the sternocostal head and incline the clavicular. It is the easiest pair to learn and the easiest to overload. Weakness: two presses overlap heavily, and the triceps/front delts limit both. Both are necessary only if you want two presses. Dropping either still leaves a decent single press.
- **C2 Flat + incline DB press.** Same coverage as C1 with more range and a freer path, but harder to get into position, harder to progress (2–2.5 kg jumps per hand) and riskier when near failure alone. Best for lifters who prefer dumbbells or have no machines.
- **C3 Horizontal press + cable fly.** Good complementarity, but **no deliberate upper-chest emphasis**. Fine if upper-chest development is not a priority.
- **C4/C5 Horizontal + incline press + fly.** The most complete coverage: sternocostal, clavicular, and a non-triceps-limited fly. Cost: three exercises compete for a beginner's 6–10 weekly chest sets, so each gets only 2–3 sets. The pec deck version (C5) edges out the cable version on ease and evidence.
- **C6 Push-up progression + incline press + cable fly.** Works, but push-up loading is harder to progress and track. Best when push-ups are needed for home days.
- **C7 Low-incline press + pec deck (added).** The incline press gives the most defensible regional tilt (upper chest) while still loading the sternocostal head heavily. In Chaves 2020, incline-only training produced no measurable deficit at the other sites compared with flat. The pec deck adds a different pattern with direct evidence of growing *both* heads (Pinto 2025). There is little overlap, it fits in two exercises, and both are easy. **Main weakness:** the lower sternal fibres get less pressing emphasis than with a flat press. The supporting evidence is EMG and acute swelling only, but it is real.

## 9.3 Top three, ranked

1. **Low-incline press + pec deck (C7).** Best two-exercise choice.
2. **Horizontal press + low-incline press + pec deck (C5).** Best three-exercise choice. Use it when time allows or when lower/mid-chest pressing matters to you.
3. **Horizontal + incline machine press (C1).** The simplest "presses only" option, best if no pec deck or cable is free.

## 9.4 Default recommendation (decisive)

> **Learn the incline chest-press machine (or low-incline dumbbell press) and the pec deck first. Add the horizontal chest-press machine as your third exercise once the first two feel routine, typically after 4–8 weeks, or straight away if you have time for ~9–12 weekly chest sets.**
>
> *If you can do only one exercise:* the horizontal chest-press machine.

## 9.5 Alternatives (and when each is better)

| Situation | Recommended set | Why, and under what conditions |

| **Prefers machines** | Incline machine press + pec deck (+ horizontal machine press) | The default already is machine-based |
| **Prefers dumbbells** | Low-incline DB press + flat DB press, + cable fly if possible | Equal hypertrophy potential (Haugen 2023). Choose if comfortable getting dumbbells into position and stopping 1–2 reps short of failure without a spotter. |

# 10. Execution guide for the leading exercises

General cues for all presses: **feet planted, shoulder blades gently pulled back and down, chest up, wrists stacked over elbows, lower the weight under control (about 2–3 s), pause briefly or touch without bouncing, press smoothly.** Breathe in on the way down and out through the press, or hold a light breath for 1–2 reps on heavier sets.

## 10.1 Horizontal chest-press machine

1. **Seat height.** Handles at **lower-to-mid chest** (roughly nipple line). Too high strains the front of the shoulder. Too low turns it into a decline press.
2. **Start depth/handles.** If adjustable, set a start position where you feel a good stretch across the chest with **no pinching at the front of the shoulder**.
3. **Grip.** Wrists straight. Use the neutral handles if offered and they feel better.
4. **Setup.** Back and head on the pad, shoulder blades back and down.
5. **Movement.** Press until the arms are nearly straight without letting the shoulders roll forward off the pad. Return slowly until you feel the stretch, then press again.
6. **Range.** Full comfortable range. Shorten it only if the shoulder hurts.
7. **Common errors.** Shoulders shrugging up, partial reps, letting the stack slam.
8. **Simplify.** Use a lighter stack and pause briefly at the stretch.
9. **Adjust if** you feel sharp front-shoulder pain (raise the seat slightly or reduce depth), or if reps slow badly with 3+ reps still in reserve (the load is too heavy for the rep range).

## 10.2 Low-incline press: machine or dumbbells

**Bench angle.** Start at **about 15–30°** (often the first or second notch). Clavicular EMG peaks around 30°, while the front delts take over at 45–60° (Rodríguez-Ridao 2020). Angles vary between benches, and **the right angle is the one that lets you feel the upper chest without shoulder discomfort.** Taller lifters with long arms may prefer the lower end.

*Machine:* set the seat so the handles start at **upper-chest/collarbone level** and press slightly up and in. Use the same cues as 10.1.

*Dumbbells* ([NASM: incline dumbbell chest press](https://nasm.org/exercise-library/two-arm-incline-dumbbell-chest-press)):

1. Sit with the dumbbells on your thighs. Lie back and use your knees to "kick" them up to chest level.
2. Forearms vertical, elbows about **30–60° from the torso** (not flared to 90°).
3. Lower until the dumbbells are beside the upper chest, with a stretch you can control.
4. Press up and slightly in. Don't clang the dumbbells together.
5. **Ending a set:** bring the dumbbells to the chest, then rock up with them onto the thighs, or lower them carefully to the sides with bent elbows. Don't drop them from straight arms.
6. **Errors.** Too heavy to get into position, bouncing, wrists bending back.
7. **Simplify.** A neutral (palms-facing) grip, a slightly lower angle, or the machine version.

## 10.3 Pec deck / machine fly

1. **Seat.** Handles or pads at **shoulder height or slightly below**, with the upper arms roughly parallel to the floor.
2. **Range limiter.** Set it so the starting position gives a stretch you can **control without strain at the front of the shoulder**. Don't set it to the maximum on day one.
3. **Arms.** Slight, *fixed* elbow bend. Lead with the elbows or pads, not the hands.
4. **Movement.** Bring the arms together in an arc until the hands or pads nearly meet. Then return slowly (about 2–3 s) into the stretch.
5. **Errors.** Shoulders rolling forward to finish the rep, heavy loads with a jerky turnaround, chasing an extreme stretch.
6. **Load.** Moderate. **Stretch under control, not maximal stretch with maximal weight.**

## 10.4 Cable fly, mid or high-to-low (pec deck alternative)

([ACE: standing chest fly](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/160/standing-chest-fly/), [ACE: standing incline cable flyes](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/163/standing-incline-cable-flyes/))

1. Pulleys at **shoulder height** (mid) or **just above** (high-to-low). For the low-to-high upper-chest version, set them near the floor.
2. Staggered stance, slight forward lean, core braced. Seated or bench versions are more stable.
3. Fixed slight elbow bend. Bring the hands together in front of the lower chest (mid/high) or the chin (low-to-high).
4. Return until you feel a controlled stretch. The cables should not pull you off balance.
5. **Errors.** Bending the elbows more as you tire, which turns it into a press; standing too close to the stack.

## 10.5 Push-up (and its progression)

([ACE: push-up](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/25/push-up/))

- **Regress:** hands on a bench or Smith bar (higher = easier), or from the knees.
- **Standard:** hands slightly wider than the shoulders, rigid plank, elbows about 30–60°, chest to roughly a fist from the floor.
- **Progress:** lower the hand height → floor → feet elevated (more upper chest) → deficit handles (more range) → plate on the back, weight vest or band → rings.
- **Rule:** if you can do more than about 20–25 reps, progress the variation so sets stay in a productive effort range.

# 11. Evidence-based chest-training parameters

Count **direct chest sets**, meaning presses and flies. Sets for other muscles that only partly involve the chest (such as overhead pressing) count as about **half a set**. The "fractional" counting method best predicted outcomes in the largest volume meta-analysis (Pelland et al., 2026).

| Variable | Practical starting point | What the evidence supports | What remains uncertain | How to adjust | Common beginner mistake |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Weekly sets** | **6–10 direct chest sets/week**, rising to 10–16 over months if recovery and progress allow | Growth increases with weekly sets with diminishing returns (Pelland 2026, 67 studies, 2,058 participants; Schoenfeld et al., 2017) | The point where returns flatten varies by person. Not chest-specific. | Add 1–2 sets/week only if performance stalls *and* you recover well | Starting at 20+ sets, or adding sets when the real problem is poor effort |
| **Sets per exercise** | 2–4 working sets | Follows from the weekly total | Optimal per-session volume | Spread across exercises and sessions | Too many exercises with 1 set each |
| **Rep range** | Presses **6–12**; fly/pec deck **10–20** | Similar growth across a wide range of loads when sets are taken near failure (Schoenfeld, Grgic et al., 2017) | Very low-load sets to failure are uncomfortable and harder to judge | Choose the range in which you can judge proximity to failure | Very heavy singles/triples for "growth" |
| **Load** | A weight that brings you within the RIR target inside the rep range | Load matters more for maximal strength than for size (Robinson et al., 2024; Schoenfeld 2017) | — | Raise load when you hit the top of the range | Ego loading that shortens range |
| **Reps in reserve (RIR)** | Presses **1–3 RIR**; machine/fly **0–2 RIR**. Use failure sparingly, on the last set of machine work. | Growth tends to improve as sets end closer to failure (Robinson et al., 2024). Momentary failure is not clearly better than stopping just short (Refalo et al., 2023). | Beginners underestimate RIR | Occasionally test a machine set to failure to calibrate | Stopping 5+ reps short ("too easy"), or grinding free-weight reps to failure without safety |
| **Rest** | **1.5–3 min** for presses, **1–2 min** for flies | Rest over ~60 s may slightly help growth. No clear extra benefit beyond ~90 s, though longer rest helps keep reps up (Singer et al., 2024). | Small effects, wide credible intervals | Rest until you can match your reps on the next set | Rushing presses with 45 s rest |
| **Frequency** | **2 sessions/week** for the chest | With volume equated, frequency itself has a negligible effect on hypertrophy (Pelland 2026). It helps spread volume and practice skill. | — | Use 3/week only to spread higher volume | Cramming all sets into one long session |
| **Progression** | **Double progression:** reach the top of the rep range on all sets at target RIR → add the smallest load step → rebuild reps | Volume-load progression is central to every successful trial | Rate of progress varies widely | Use small increments (machine pin or 1–2.5 kg) | Adding weight every session regardless of reps |
| **Range of motion** | Full comfortable range, with emphasis on control in the stretched position | Full ROM trivially better than partial overall; lengthened partials *may* be useful (Wolf et al., 2023, not chest-specific) | Chest-specific ROM trials (a lengthened-partial chest study exists in conference proceedings but could not be verified here) | Shorten the range only for comfort | Half reps with heavy loads |
| **Training at long lengths** | Don't cut the bottom of the press or the stretch of the fly | Consistent with the ROM data above | Magnitude for the pecs | Add lengthened partials at the end of a set only once you're experienced (optional) | Dangerous stretch on dumbbell flies |
| **Exercise order** | Priority press first (incline for upper-chest focus), fly last | Exercises done first in a session generally perform better **[E]** | — | Rotate order across sessions if you like | Fly to failure before heavy pressing |
| **Recovery** | 48–72 h between chest sessions | Practical norm **[E]** | Individual | If reps drop from one session to the next, cut 1–2 sets | Training a still-sore chest at high effort |
| **Plateaus** | 2–3 sessions with no rep or load gain | — | — | Check sleep, effort and technique first. Then add 1–2 sets or change the rep range. Deload (halve sets for a week) if fatigued. | Changing exercises every week |
| **When *not* to add volume** | Performance rising, or recovery poor | Diminishing returns (Pelland 2026) | — | Keep volume while progress continues | "More is always better" |

**A simple weekly template using the default recommendation** (two chest sessions inside whatever split you already use):

- **Session A:** Incline machine or DB press 3 × 8–12 → Pec deck 2–3 × 12–15
- **Session B:** Horizontal machine press 3 × 8–12 *(the third exercise, once added)* → Pec deck 2 × 12–15
- That is about 10–11 direct chest sets per week. Beginners can start with 2 sets each and build up.

# 12. Safety and modification guide

| Issue | Applies most to | Reasonable modification | When to switch exercise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excessive load | All, especially barbell and DB | Use a load you can take through full range at RIR 2–3 with consistent reps | — |
| Poor control | DB press, DB fly, cables | Use a slower lowering phase, less load, or a machine | If control doesn't improve after a few sessions, use the machine version |
| Uncomfortable shoulder extension (front-of-shoulder pinch at the bottom) | Deep DB press, dips, pec deck at a far range setting | Reduce depth, use a neutral grip, tuck the elbows to ~30–45°, lower the incline, shorten the pec-deck range | If it persists despite changes, try another exercise (e.g., machine → DB or vice versa) |
| Grip or elbow position | All presses | Wrists stacked, elbows not flared to 90° | — |
| Unstable setup | DB getting-into-position, standing cables | Use the knee kick-up, staggered stance or seated cable version | Use a machine |
| Incorrect machine adjustment | Machines | Seat so the handles sit at mid-chest (flat) or upper chest (incline). Set a controlled start range. | — |
| Not using safety stops | Barbell, Smith | **Always set safety arms/catches** just below chest depth | — |
| No spotter on demanding free-weight sets | Barbell, heavy DB | Stop 2–3 reps short, or use machines for near-failure sets | — |
| Excessive volume | All | Start at 6–10 sets/week | — |
| Abrupt progression | All | Smallest load step only after hitting the rep target on all sets | — |
| **Persistent or sharp joint pain** | All | **Stop the exercise.** | See below |

**Ordinary effort or a reason to stop?** Burning, muscle fatigue and next-day muscle soreness are normal. **Stop and seek a professional assessment** for sharp, stabbing or localized joint pain; pain that persists after the set or worsens session to session; a sudden "pop" or bruising in the chest or armpit; numbness or tingling; or loss of strength. A sudden pop with bruising can indicate a pectoral tendon injury, which is classically associated with heavy bench pressing (Haley & Zacchilli, 2014). This guide does not diagnose, and **no exercise is guaranteed to prevent injury.**

# 15. Research limitations and independent quality audit

## 15.1 What this research cannot establish

- **Head-to-head chest hypertrophy trials are scarce.** No longitudinal studies were found comparing machine vs DB vs barbell chest pressing specifically, cable vs DB fly, decline vs flat, or converging vs fixed machines. Those comparisons rely on general meta-analyses, biomechanics or expert judgment.
- **Regional evidence is thin.** The upper-chest conclusion rests on one 8-week trial in 47 untrained men training once a week, with a surprisingly large effect. The acute swelling data (Albarello 2022) partly conflict with it.
- **Populations.** Most participants are young, untrained or recreationally trained men. Generalization to women, older adults and people with shoulder history is inference.
- **Duration.** Studies run 4–12 weeks. Long-term (multi-year) differences between exercises are unknown.
- **Measurement.** Ultrasound thickness at one or a few sites may miss regional changes. Only Lanza 2024 used MRI.
- **Beginner scores are expert judgments.** The weighted model is transparent but subjective. Change the weights and the order among the tied middle group changes.

## 15.3 Final answer to the success question

*"If a beginner wants to develop the entire pectoralis major in a well-equipped gym, which two or three exercises should they learn first?"*

**A low-incline press (machine or dumbbell) and a pec deck, then a horizontal chest-press machine as the third.** The incline press gives the best-supported upper-chest emphasis while still heavily loading the mid chest. The pec deck adds a different pattern with direct evidence of growing both main heads, without the triceps cutting sets short. The flat machine press closes the remaining gap on mid- and lower-sternal pressing and is the easiest exercise in the gym to overload safely. The evidence is solid that these *kinds* of exercises work. It is much weaker on exactly which of several similar machines or dumbbells is best. For that reason, the effort, volume and progression rules in Section 11 matter more than which variant is chosen.
