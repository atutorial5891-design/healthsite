Scope. Lower-body training only: hips, glutes, thighs, knees, lower legs, ankles, feet and toes, for use as a long-term reference in a well-equipped commercial gym. Nutrition, diet, protein, supplements, calories and meal planning are deliberately excluded. Nothing here is medical advice. Sharp, persistent or worsening joint pain needs a physiotherapist or doctor, not a different exercise.

1. Executive overview

The answer in one paragraph. A complete lower body is built from five movement patterns plus three small “gap” groups that most leg days miss. The patterns are: (1) a squat or leg press (quads, glutes, adductors), (2) a hip hinge such as the Romanian deadlift (hamstrings, glutes, adductor magnus), (3) a knee-flexion curl, ideally seated (hamstrings at long length), (4) a single-leg movement such as the Bulgarian split squat or step-up (hip and knee control), and (5) a calf raise. The gaps are the hip abductors and rotators (gluteus medius/minimus, deep six), the shin and ankle stabilisers (tibialis anterior/posterior, fibularis) and the intrinsic foot and toe muscles. A short accessory block of hip abduction, tibialis raises, banded ankle work, a Copenhagen plank and the short-foot exercise closes almost all of them.

If you only remember one routine: leg press or hack squat → Romanian deadlift → seated leg curl → Bulgarian split squat → standing calf raise, plus 10 minutes of hip abduction, tibialis raises and short-foot work twice a week.

How confident is this?

Conclusion Evidence type Confidence
Multi-joint squat/press patterns grow the quadriceps, glutes and adductors Direct (many RCTs, MRI/ultrasound) High
Deeper squats grow the glutes and adductors more than half squats; quads grow similarly Direct (Kubo et al., 2019) Moderate
Seated leg curls grow the hamstrings more than lying leg curls Direct (Maeo et al., 2021) Moderate
The rectus femoris needs knee extension (leg extension) work in addition to squats Direct: squat training did not significantly grow it (Kubo et al., 2019); knee-extension training does Moderate
Hip thrusts and squats produce similar glute growth Direct (Plotkin et al., 2023, untrained) Moderate
Training muscles at long lengths is at least as good as, and often better than, short-length partials Direct, several small trials (Pedrosa 2022; Kassiano 2023) Moderate, still emerging
The Nordic hamstring exercise reduces hamstring injuries Meta-analysis (van Dyk 2019), later disputed on methods Moderate–low
The Copenhagen adduction programme reduces groin problems in footballers Cluster RCT (Harøy 2019) Moderate (population-specific)
Short-foot and toe exercises strengthen foot intrinsics and improve arch control Small trials (e.g., Mulligan & Cook 2013) Low–moderate
Foot position on the leg press or squat can “isolate” the inner/outer quad Not supported —

Bottom line on certainty. The patterns above rest on good evidence. The exact choice between similar machines, bars and dumbbells does not, and effort, progression and consistency matter more than which variant you choose.

2.9 Functions of the big toe and lesser toes

Toe(s) Joints Muscles Function in movement Practical training
Big toe (hallux) MTP + one IP joint FHL, FHB, EHL, EHB, abductor and adductor hallucis Takes a large share of forefoot load at push-off; its extension (about 50–60° needed in walking) winds up the plantar fascia (“windlass mechanism”) and stiffens the arch Big-toe press with lesser toes lifted; calf raises finishing over the big toe; big-toe mobility
Second–fifth toes MTP + two IP joints each FDL, FDB, EDL, EDB, lumbricals, interossei, abductor digiti minimi Widen the base of support, help balance, share push-off load; lumbricals keep toes straight instead of clawing Lesser-toe press with big toe lifted (toe yoga); toe spreading; towel curls

Evidence note: toe flexor strength has been associated with balance and fall risk in older adults in observational research, but that does not prove that training it improves performance in young lifters. The training here is low-cost insurance rather than a proven performance tool.

2.10 Research method and evidence hierarchy

  1. Direct long-term evidence: training studies measuring muscle size (MRI/ultrasound) or strength in the specific muscle.
  2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses pooling those studies.
  3. Acute evidence: EMG, muscle swelling, moment-arm modelling. Useful for hypotheses, never sufficient by itself for ranking growth.
  4. Anatomy and biomechanics: line of pull, moment arms, muscle length.
  5. Expert coaching opinion: labelled as such.

Where only levels 3–5 support a claim, it is marked (coaching consensus) or (biomechanical inference).

3.1 Barbell Hip Thrust

Category: Compound · Barbell · Bilateral · Level: Beginner → Advanced

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus maximus
  • Secondary: Hamstrings, adductor magnus, quadriceps (modest), gluteus medius
  • Specific muscles: Gluteus maximus (upper and lower fibres), semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long head, adductor magnus posterior head
  • Joint actions: Hip extension (main), slight posterior pelvic tilt at lockout; knee held at ~90°

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
No — Optional (light substitute) Yes No Optional (around knees) Glute bridge / single-leg hip thrust

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Upper back (just below shoulder blades) on a bench ~40 cm high, chin slightly tucked, ribs down.
  • Foot position: Flat, about hip-width, toes slightly out; shins vertical at the top of the rep.
  • Hands / grip: Hands just outside the hips holding the bar; use a thick bar pad.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: Roll the bar over the hips while seated; a hip-thrust bench with a belt makes setup easier.
  • Starting range: Hips just off the floor, torso and shins forming a ‘V’.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Seated, bar over hip crease, upper back on the bench edge.
  2. Initiate. Drive through the whole foot (heels and mid-foot) and squeeze the glutes before the bar moves.
  3. Movement path. Hips rise vertically; the upper back pivots on the bench while the chin stays tucked so the ribs and pelvis move together.
  4. Breathing. Breathe in and brace before each rep; exhale near lockout.
  5. Contraction. Peak glute contraction as the hips reach full extension.
  6. End position. Torso parallel to floor, knees ~90°, shins vertical, slight posterior pelvic tilt — no arching of the low back.
  7. Return. Lower under control over 2 s until the hips are close to the floor.
  8. Recommended ROM. Full hip extension at top to ~60–90° hip flexion at bottom.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Knees track over the 2nd–3rd toes; chin and ribs tucked; don’t hyperextend the lumbar spine to ‘finish’.
  • Tempo: 1 s up, 1 s pause at top, 2 s down.
  • Muscle engagement: If you feel mostly hamstrings, move the feet closer; if mostly quads, move them further away.
Common mistake How to correct it
Arching the lower back at the top Tuck chin, keep ribs down, finish with the glutes (slight posterior tilt)
Bench too high or too low Bench edge should sit at the bottom of the shoulder blades
Pushing through toes Keep heels down; lift toes slightly if needed
Bar slides or hurts Use a thick pad or a hip-thrust belt machine

F. Training parameters

Beginner Intermediate Advanced Rest Progression
2–3 × 10–15, light 3–4 × 8–12 3–5 × 6–12, plus pauses or single-leg work 2–3 min Add reps to the top of the range, then add 5–10 kg

  • Advantages: Heavy glute loading with little spinal compression or balance demand; easy to overload
  • Disadvantages: Awkward setup; glutes are loaded most at short length, so pair with a deep squat or split squat

3.2 Glute Bridge (bodyweight or dumbbell)

Category: Bodyweight / Dumbbell · Bilateral · Level: Beginner

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus maximus
  • Secondary: Hamstrings, adductor magnus
  • Specific muscles: Gluteus maximus, semitendinosus, biceps femoris long head
  • Joint actions: Hip extension from the floor

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
No — Optional Optional No Optional (above knees) Is bodyweight

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Lying on back, arms at sides, ribs down.
  • Foot position: Flat, hip-width, heels ~20–30 cm from hips.
  • Hands / grip: Palms on the floor, or holding a dumbbell on the hips.
  • Starting range: Hips on the floor.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Lie with knees bent and feet flat.
  2. Initiate. Press heels into the floor and squeeze the glutes.
  3. Movement path. Lift the hips in a straight line until knees, hips and shoulders align.
  4. Breathing. Exhale on the way up.
  5. Contraction. Hold 1–2 s with the glutes tight.
  6. End position. Straight line from knees to shoulders; no low-back arch.
  7. Return. Lower slowly to touch the floor.
  8. Recommended ROM. Floor to full hip extension.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Knees stay hip-width (a band helps).
  • Tempo: 1 s up, 2 s hold, 2 s down.
  • Muscle engagement: Think ‘push the floor away with the heels’.

3.3 Cable Glute Kickback

Category: Isolation · Cable · Unilateral · Level: Beginner → Intermediate

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus maximus
  • Secondary: Hamstrings, gluteus medius (stabilising leg)
  • Specific muscles: Gluteus maximus (mainly upper/middle fibres when kicking slightly out to the side)
  • Joint actions: Hip extension (with slight abduction if the leg travels back-and-out)

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
Yes Cable pulley (low position) or dedicated glute-kickback machine No No Yes Alternative Quadruped donkey kick

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Hinge forward ~30–45°, hands on the cable frame, spine neutral.
  • Foot position: Ankle cuff on the working leg; stand on the other leg with a soft knee.
  • Hands / grip: Hold the frame for balance.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: Pulley at the lowest setting.
  • Starting range: Working hip flexed ~30–45° (foot slightly in front).

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Hinge, brace, working foot slightly forward.
  2. Initiate. Start by squeezing the glute, not by arching the back.
  3. Movement path. Kick the leg back and slightly out in an arc, knee slightly bent.
  4. Breathing. Exhale as the leg goes back.
  5. Contraction. Squeeze at full hip extension.
  6. End position. Thigh just behind the body line — stop before the lower back arches.
  7. Return. Return under control until the hip is flexed again.
  8. Recommended ROM. From ~45° hip flexion to just past neutral.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Pelvis stays square; no rotation.
  • Tempo: 1 s back, 1 s squeeze, 2 s return.
  • Muscle engagement: A forward torso lean allows more hip flexion at the start (longer glute length).

Swinging from the lower back Reduce load; stop at neutral hip extension
Rotating the pelvis open Keep hips square to the machine
Standing upright Lean forward so the glute is stretched at the start

3.4 Seated Hip Abduction (machine)

Category: Isolation · Machine · Level: Beginner

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus medius, gluteus minimus
  • Secondary: Upper gluteus maximus, TFL
  • Specific muscles: Gluteus medius (all fibres), gluteus minimus, superior fibres of gluteus maximus, TFL, piriformis (hip flexed)
  • Joint actions: Hip abduction (with the hip flexed ~90° when seated)

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
Yes Hip abduction machine (outer thigh) No No Alternative Alternative Side-lying hip abduction

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Seated upright, back on the pad; a slight forward lean shifts emphasis to upper glute max.
  • Foot position: On the foot rests.
  • Hands / grip: On the handles.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: Pads on the outside of the knees; set the start so legs are close together with tension.
  • Starting range: Thighs together.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Sit tall, pads outside knees.
  2. Initiate. Push the knees out by squeezing the side of the hips.
  3. Movement path. Move the thighs outward in an arc.
  4. Breathing. Exhale out, inhale back.
  5. Contraction. Squeeze at the widest point.
  6. End position. As wide as you can control.
  7. Return. Return slowly; don’t let the weight stack slam.
  8. Recommended ROM. Full available range; the inner (thighs-together) position matters.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Hips stay in the seat; no bouncing.
  • Tempo: 1 s out, 1 s hold, 2–3 s in.
  • Muscle engagement: Leaning forward (~30°) tends to increase glute max involvement (coaching consensus).

3.5 Side-Lying Hip Abduction

Category: Bodyweight · Isolation · Rehab-oriented · Level: Beginner

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus medius
  • Secondary: Gluteus minimus, TFL
  • Specific muscles: Posterior and middle gluteus medius (when the leg is slightly behind the body)
  • Joint actions: Hip abduction with the hip extended

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
No — No No No Optional Is bodyweight

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Lying on side, body in a straight line, bottom knee bent for stability.
  • Foot position: Top leg straight, toes pointing forward or slightly down.
  • Hands / grip: Top hand on the hip to monitor rolling.
  • Starting range: Top leg resting on bottom leg.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Lie on your side, pelvis stacked.
  2. Initiate. Lift the top leg by squeezing the side of the hip.
  3. Movement path. Raise the leg slightly behind the body line, heel leading.
  4. Breathing. Exhale on the lift.
  5. Contraction. Hold 1–2 s at about 30–40°.
  6. End position. Leg in line with or slightly behind the torso.
  7. Return. Lower slowly without resting.
  8. Recommended ROM. ~0–40° abduction (more means the trunk is side-bending).

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Don’t roll back; don’t let the leg drift forward (that turns it into TFL/hip-flexor work).
  • Tempo: 1 s up, 2 s hold, 2 s down.
  • Muscle engagement: EMG studies rank this among the highest-activation glute medius exercises (Distefano et al., 2009).

3.6 Banded Lateral Walk (Monster Walk)

Category: Resistance band · Functional · Activation · Level: Beginner

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gluteus medius
  • Secondary: Gluteus minimus, TFL, upper glute max, quadriceps (isometric)
  • Specific muscles: Gluteus medius of both the moving and the stance leg
  • Joint actions: Hip abduction and isometric hip/knee stability in a quarter squat

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
No — No No No Yes (mini-band above knees or around ankles/feet) Lateral shuffle without band

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Quarter squat, chest up, hips back slightly.
  • Foot position: Hip-width, toes forward; band tension already present.
  • Hands / grip: On hips or in front.
  • Starting range: Feet hip-width.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Place a mini-band above the knees (easier) or around the forefeet (harder), feet hip-width.
  2. Initiate. Sit into a quarter squat and push the lead knee out against the band.
  3. Movement path. Step sideways 20–30 cm with the lead foot, then follow with the trailing foot only far enough to keep tension.
  4. Breathing. Breathe steadily; keep the trunk braced.
  5. Contraction. Feel the side of both hips — the stance-side glute medius works as hard as the stepping side.
  6. End position. Feet never come fully together; torso stays level without rocking.
  7. Return. Take the same number of steps back in the other direction.
  8. Recommended ROM. 10–15 steps each way per set.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Knees stay over toes; torso level, no rocking side to side.
  • Tempo: Slow, controlled steps.
  • Muscle engagement: Band around the feet raises glute activation compared with above the knees (biomechanical/EMG evidence).

Knees caving Push knees out into the band
Feet snapping together Keep band taut throughout

4.1 Barbell Back Squat

Category: Compound · Barbell · Bilateral · Level: Intermediate → Advanced

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Quadriceps, gluteus maximus, adductor magnus
  • Secondary: Hamstrings (stabilisers), erector spinae, calves, core
  • Specific muscles: Vastus lateralis, medialis, intermedius (rectus femoris less), glute max, adductor magnus
  • Joint actions: Knee extension, hip extension, ankle dorsiflexion (descent) / plantarflexion

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
Yes Squat rack / power rack with safety pins No Yes No No Bodyweight squat

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Bar on upper traps (high bar) or rear delts (low bar), upper back tight, chest up.
  • Foot position: Shoulder-width or slightly wider, toes out 15–30°.
  • Hands / grip: Just outside shoulders, wrists straight, elbows under/behind bar.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: J-hooks at mid-chest; safety pins just below bottom depth.
  • Starting range: Standing tall after a two-step walkout.

D. Execution and form

  1. Start. Unrack, two to three steps back, feet set.
  2. Initiate. Big breath into the belly, brace, then break at hips and knees together.
  3. Movement path. Sit down between the hips; knees travel forward and out over the toes; bar stays over mid-foot.
  4. Breathing. Hold the breath down and through the hardest part; exhale near the top.
  5. Contraction. Drive up through the whole foot, keeping the chest and hips rising together.
  6. End position. Stand tall with hips and knees locked.
  7. Return. Re-brace before the next rep.
  8. Recommended ROM. As deep as you can keep a neutral spine and heels down — usually at or below parallel.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Knees in line with toes; bar path vertical over mid-foot; neutral spine.
  • Tempo: 2–3 s down, controlled bounce-free bottom, drive up.
  • Muscle engagement: Knees moving past toes is normal and safe for healthy knees; restricting it shifts stress to the hips and lower back (Fry et al., 2003).

Knees caving in (valgus) Cue ‘knees out’; lighter load; strengthen glute medius
Heels lifting Improve ankle mobility or use heel-elevated shoes/plates
Hips shooting up first (‘good-morning squat’) Lighter load, pause squats, stronger quads
Losing brace at the bottom Breathe and brace before every rep; consider a belt for heavy sets

  • Disadvantages: Technically demanding; low back and mobility can limit it; rectus femoris grows little

4.2 Barbell Front Squat

Category: Compound · Barbell · Level: Intermediate → Advanced

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Quadriceps
  • Secondary: Gluteus maximus, adductors, upper back, core
  • Specific muscles: Vasti; glute max and adductor magnus (slightly less than back squat)
  • Joint actions: Knee extension, hip extension

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
Yes Squat rack No Yes No No Goblet squat

C. Starting position

  • Body posture: Bar on front delts touching the throat, elbows high, torso upright.
  • Foot position: Shoulder-width, toes slightly out.
  • Hands / grip: Clean grip (fingertips) or crossed arms; straps around the bar are an option.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: Rack at mid-chest.
  • Starting range: Standing.

D. Execution and form

  5. Contraction. Drive up leading with the elbows so the chest stays tall and the bar doesn’t roll forward.
  6. End position. Stand tall with hips and knees extended.
  7. Return. Re-brace before each rep.
  8. Recommended ROM. As deep as an upright torso allows — usually below parallel.

E. Technique details

  • Posture and alignment: Elbows high so the bar doesn’t roll forward.
  • Tempo: 2–3 s down, no bounce, drive up.
  • Muscle engagement: More upright torso means less hip and spinal load and relatively more knee demand.

7. Knee strength and stability

No single muscle ‘stabilises the knee’. Knee control comes from the quadriceps and hamstrings (co-contraction), the gastrocnemius and popliteus (rotational control and unlocking from full extension), and the glute medius and external rotators above it, which limit inward knee collapse. Practical knee strength therefore means: full-range quad work (Chapter 4), hamstring curls (Chapter 5), hip abduction (Chapter 3) and the single-leg patterns below. The ‘VMO’ cannot be isolated by foot position or short-range work; it grows with any heavy quad training. For irritable knees, the Spanish squat (4.10) and leg extension with a controlled range (4.6) are well-tolerated options.

8.1 Standing Calf Raise (machine or Smith)

Category: Isolation · Machine · Level: Beginner → Advanced

A. Anatomy

  • Primary: Gastrocnemius (both heads)
  • Secondary: Soleus, deep plantarflexors
  • Specific muscles: Medial and lateral gastrocnemius, soleus, FHL, tibialis posterior, fibularis
  • Joint actions: Ankle plantarflexion with straight knee

B. Equipment

Machine Exact machine Dumbbell Barbell Cable Band Bodyweight alternative
Yes Standing calf raise machine (shoulder pads) or Smith machine + step Alternative No No No Single-leg calf raise on a step

  • Body posture: Standing tall under the pads, knees straight but not locked.
  • Foot position: Balls of the feet on the platform edge, hip-width, toes forward.
  • Hands / grip: On handles.
  • Machine / setup adjustments: Shoulder pad height so you start at a deep stretch.

14. Exercise variations and biomechanics

14.1 Why variations produce different demands

Factor What it changes Practical example
Joint angle Which joint does more work and which muscles have the better leverage A more upright torso (front squat, hack squat) increases knee demand; more forward lean (low-bar squat, long-stride lunge) increases hip demand
Muscle length Two-joint muscles are lengthened or shortened by the other joint’s position Seated leg curl (hip flexed) keeps hamstrings long; the squat shortens rectus femoris at the hip while lengthening it at the knee, so it barely changes length
Loading (resistance) profile Where in the range the exercise is hardest Hip thrust is hardest at the top (short glute); deep squat and split squat are hardest near the bottom (long glute)
Stability How much load you can use vs how much balance you must provide Machines let you approach failure safely; free-weight single-leg work trains control but caps load
Range of motion Total stretch and stimulus Full and lengthened-range training is generally at least as good as partials at short lengths (Pedrosa 2022; Kassiano 2023)
Difficulty Technique learning time, fatigue Leg press < goblet squat < back squat; Nordic curl is very hard
Equipment Access Dumbbells everywhere; GHD, reverse hyper and pendulum squat are rare

16. Beginner-to-advanced progression

Stage Duration (typical) Focus Exercise types Move up when…
1. Beginner 0–6 months Learn patterns, controlled tempo, stable machines Leg press, goblet squat, leg extension, seated/lying curl, DB RDL, glute bridge, hip abduction, calf machine, tibialis raise, short-foot, balance You can perform every exercise with consistent form for 3 × 12 at a weight that feels hard (≈2 reps in reserve), and you’ve added load steadily for 8–12 weeks
2. Intermediate 6–24 months Dumbbells, unilateral work, progressive overload, targeted isolation Hack squat or back squat, BSS, walking lunge, barbell RDL, hip thrust, Copenhagen, single-leg calf raise, banded ankle work You squat/press and hinge with stable technique near failure, progress is slowing week to week, and you can manage 12–20 hard sets per muscle per week
3. Advanced 2+ years Heavier loads, specialised variations, periodised blocks Front/pause squats, deficit BSS, Nordic and GHR, single-leg RDL heavy, lengthened partials, weighted reverse Nordic, tib bar Progress requires planned blocks (e.g., 4–6 weeks of accumulation, then a deload)

Progression rules (all stages). Use double progression: work within a rep range (e.g., 8–12); when all sets reach the top, add the smallest load step. Train most sets within 0–3 reps of failure. Deload (halve the sets) every 6–10 weeks or when performance falls two sessions running.

Strength training is associated with improved running economy (Balsalobre-Fernández et al., 2016; Blagrove et al., 2018) and with fewer sports injuries overall (Lauersen et al., 2014). For runners, the most transferable choices are single-leg work (split squat, step-up, single-leg RDL), heavy calf work (both straight and bent knee), hamstring eccentrics and foot/ankle stability. This document does not include a running programme.

22. Final recommendations

  1. Build around five patterns: squat/press, hinge, leg curl, single-leg, calf raise.
  2. Add leg extensions if you want complete quadriceps development — squats underdevelop the rectus femoris.
  3. Choose the seated leg curl over the lying curl when both are available.
  4. Train calves with a stretch pause, and include a bent-knee (seated) version for the soleus.
  5. Add one hip-abduction exercise every leg day. It’s the single biggest gap in most programs.
  6. Do the 20-minute foot-and-ankle session twice a week; it’s low effort and fills nearly every remaining gap in the coverage matrix.
  7. Progress conservatively with double progression and deload every 6–10 weeks.
  8. Prioritise effort and consistency over finding the “perfect” variation; similar exercises are close to ties.
  9. Use the videos as technique checks, starting with Squat University for squats and hinges, E3 Rehab for Nordic, Copenhagen and leg extensions, and Jeff Nippard for evidence context.
